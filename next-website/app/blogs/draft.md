# My Journey as a Teen YT Content Creator

Alright guys! I just realized that it's been 10+ years since I last published my YouTube video to the world! Looking back over my 2 year YouTube history, I have experienced the ups and downs of my YouTube journey, ever since I have uploaded my first video onto the platform at only 13 years of age — the age that I can be legal to post on YouTube independently. At the same time, these things have had a dramatic impact on my life and it taught me so many things at such a young age that I wouldn't have imagined I would pick up without it. Knowing that it was a thing that only some teens at the time had the privilege of expressing their 'creative' side of themselves through platforms like YouTube, there were numerous perks and challenges faced along the way.

### Why I decided to have a YT Channel

One of the main reasons why I wanted to run a YouTube channel was because I got deeply inspired by numerous YouTubers that I looked up to, such as DanTDM, CaptainSparklez, StampyLongHead and so many more, to the point where I initially wanted to make YouTube my career. Because of this, the process of me making a single YouTube video felt extremely enjoyable for me where I could not wait to share myself with the world. For my first ever gaming channel, I invented the name PixelHyperCube, where the words:

- <b>Pixel</b> - Indicates my fascination with pixels and pixel art, 
- <b>Hyper</b> -  Symbolizes my enthusiasm and my hyperactive personality as a kid, 
- <b>Cube</b> - Acknowledges my fascination in things that are cubic, such as the cubes in Minecraft and Rubik's Cubes. From there, my YouTube journey took off.

You might also think that I have invented a truly nerdy name related to hypercubes (a geometric shape that extends an idea of a square and a cube into n dimensions), but to be honest, I did not think about that at all. I just wanted to combine these 3 words into something that best describes me at the time.

### PixelHyperCube - my original channel

It was July the 4th, not only the day I decided to create PixelHyperCube, but it was also the US's independence day. For my first video, I wanted to create a hook for new viewers to identify me as a Minecrafter and speedcuber, so I thought about making a video of me solving Sethbling's Rubik's Cube in Minecraft. I spent a total of 10 and a half minutes to solve the entire thing, and to be honest, it felt like a good challenge even though I had messed up some algorithms during the solve. However, looking back, it was extremely normal to cringe at super old videos like these, where I had made numerous mistakes that would instantly make viewers bored and uninterested in watching the video:

- There was pretty much zero editing done in the entire video, and it was very obvious that I did not use any form of media editor like iMovie (I was using my iMac at the time) to trim videos. I just recorded straight from QuickTime.
- Even though I legitimately had a timer that I was about to record my times with, I did not even leverage it at all (I did acknowledge it in my YT Bio).
- Since I had done zero editing, I did not even speed up any important portions, even though I had put the timestamps in the description, and that I did not put any form of text and graphics (B-roll) onto the video to make viewers accurately detect what they're seeing.
- I coughed numerous times in the video which might annoy viewers a lot (I had sinus issues at the time 🙁)

… and the list goes on.

So after making this, I began to quickly make newer videos that range from various Rubik's Cube solves as well as more Minecraft content. However, the most viewed video on my channel is my longest rollercoaster I made in Minecraft, where it amassed ~6.2K views. Looking back, I had cringed numerous times because I received numerous constructive feedback about viewers feeling bored about me constructing those infinitely long spirals down that was the main reason why the rollercoaster was 45 damn minutes long! Surprisingly, and thankfully, I did not get a lot of hate or nasty comments on my channel, even though YouTube's comment moderation process was notably unstable without AI detectors that would filter out nasty comments. Also, throughout the 45 minutes of Minecraft rollercoaster footage, I should have seriously prioritized viewer engagement and retention, which was something that I did not even think of in the first place. Other than that, I began to upload more interesting Minecraft and Agar.io content, such as creating interesting tutorials to make more fun stuff in Minecraft and Agar.io, as well as showcasing more of my interesting creations in Minecraft.

### The Splitting of PixelHyperCuber from PixelHyperCube

However, after I had been uploading more speedcubing content onto my original channel, I realized a flaw that would make my subscribers and viewers confused — gaming (Minecraft/Agar.io) viewers might feel 'disappointed' whenever I upload a Rubik's Cube video, and vice versa. So, I made an announcement regarding the split of my channel from the original PixelHyperCube channel, where I made another separate channel, PixelHyperCuber, a safe haven for me to express my deep interest in speedcubing and twisty puzzle collecting. In that way, speedcubing viewers can easily flock over to PixelHyperCuber to experience and watch my speedcubing videos, while gaming viewers can still remain back on PixelHyperCube to watch my YouTube videos.

#### PixelHyperCuber (PHCuber):

PixelHyperCuber (or PHCuber for short) is a YouTube channel that is dedicated to my interest in Rubik's Cubes and twisty puzzle collecting. Below is a summary of what my channel was about from my first channel's intro video I made back in 2016, which you can read from the script down below:

<blockquote><i>Hey guys, I'm PixelHyperCuber, also known as PHCuber. This channel is dedicated to speedcubers and people who would like to know more about cubing. I'm a speedcuber and I also love puzzle collecting. On this channel I usually do unboxings, reviews, tutorials and more fun stuff including solves from competitions. I usually post videos 1-2 times per week and it depends whether I'm free or not. If you have any questions or suggestions to improve on my channel you can leave it down below in the comments section in any of my videos. If you're finding me on social media you can like me on Facebook, follow me on Instagram and Twitter and the links to all of those will be in the description. If you really like my videos subscribe to stay tuned.</i></blockquote>

So yeah, looking back at this particular script I made in mid-2016, I would say that it defined who I was at that certain period of time — I was very interested in sharing my passion in speedcubing and my fascination with twisty puzzles to the world by uploading YT videos and posting Rubik's Cube photos and videos on my IG page. The one thing that stood out to me at the time was that I explicitly stated that I accepted constructive feedback from viewers on how to improve my videos to further achieve my goal of increasing viewer satisfaction and retention —  the things I prioritized over the raw number of views and subscribers after making the rookie mistake of 'begging' for people to subscribe to my channel numerous times before. I will explain each category of the videos I uploaded onto my channel, alongside what so many other YouCubers (speedcubing/twisty puzzle YouTubers) did during that time.

##### Unboxings

For unboxings, I had to source cubes from numerous online cube stores worldwide way before modern platforms like Shopee and Lazada rose in prominence. I began ordering them from stores like TheCubicle, Lighttake and Cubezz, one of the world's most well known speedcube and twisty puzzle stores worldwide. Usually, most of the parcels have arrived on time, except for some where one order from TheCubicle got delayed severely for about a month because they were moving warehouse and they had to wait for new cubes to arrive in the shop. So far, I had a mostly positive experience with all of them, except for some parts of the customer service when they attempted to dodge certain responsibilities when they couldn't find a solution for the later arrival of my parcel. The puzzles I unboxed usually included newly designed cubes (especially those newly popular ones after release) so that I could quickly share my thoughts with cubers who are hyping over these cubes. In addition, I unboxed more interesting stuff that ranges from 9x9s, minx puzzles, accessories such as lubes and custom stickers, and so much more. Unboxing all of them was genuinely quite a fun experience for me to see it with my own naked eyes as I could feel an immediate sense of excitement after feeling and testing a new puzzle for the very first time. It got so fascinating to the point where I said 'It's a great cube' even though I tested some cubes that didn't give me a good first impression out of the box (such as pieces falling out after doing my first turns)!

##### Reviews

My reviews went through a structured format that was a 'template' used by numerous YouCubers to maintain organizational and professional standards:


One example:
General Info -> Performance (Turning, Speed, Corner Cutting, etc.) -> Mechanical Structure & Design -> Flaws -> Final Thoughts

I would say that even though this progression felt 'unoriginal' that so many YouCubers and some professional speedcubers use, it made sense as to why it's formatted this way. It made it easy for viewers to understand the cube much better and it eliminates a lot of unnecessary rambling and disorganization that sort of happens amongst those who were probably making these for the first time. This was the template that JRCuber used and I took a lot of inspiration into how he makes these videos, so I followed his style of videos for a long while. 

For my first ever review on the Gans 356S V2 Advanced (one of GAN's latest cubes at the start of 2016), the fact that I followed this review flowchart effectively made it surprisingly functional even though the video had some of these noticeable flaws:
- The lack of white balance (resulting in a dimly lit yellowish tone in some shots) 
- How the lamp was positioned while recording
- The way I had been reading the script that sort of sounded like I was taking an oral exam.
- Covering parts of the timer when I was solving the cube
- The outro music being deafeningly loud at the end of the video

Because of these flaws, the unfortunate reality is that some viewers might not take me too seriously because viewers prioritize video quality as it shows the cube more clearly in the video. So, as a result, they might instantly click away from the video when they are not very happy with the fact that I had been filming it in this way. And after I got some more constructive feedback from my viewers, I had tried my best to take them into consideration (which I managed to do — hopefully!). Thanks to my parents' support for helping me get new filming equipment and getting new cubes, I had managed to overcome several obstacles for making a great cubing video, especially for a review.

##### Tutorials

To be frank, I wasn't really the best at making tutorial videos, but my notable ones include 3x3 PLL (Permutation of the Last Layer — the very last method to solving the 3x3 Rubik's Cube) algorithms, a full 2x2 tutorial, and re-stickering puzzles. However, looking back at it, I would have likely taken these things with more caution, as I have to understand that I am helping those who don't even know the skill in the first place. Knowing that I made these tutorials showed that I was trying to extend a helping hand to those who are keen on picking up new cubing knowledge.

##### Competition Vlogs

During my YouTube career, I had the opportunity to participate in 2 official WCA competitions — particularly the Singapore Open 2016 and the Singapore 333 Cube Competition 2016. To be honest, I did not join the competition because I wanted to get a podium placement (my times didn't really reflect it at the time), but I desired to socialize and interact with more members in the cubing community because it was my first time entering an official WCA competition. One of the main reasons why I decided to attend official WCA competitions was because I got intrigued by MMAP's 2013 World Championship video where he showcased numerous fun and interesting moments that happened during the lengthy 3-day competition, and that I wanted to replicate what I saw during the competition itself.

###### Singapore Open 2016

This was my first ever time I had ever participated in an official WCA competition, where I signed up for events that ranged from the 2x2 to 5x5, as well as 3x3 one-handed, pyraminx (a 4-sided, triangle-shaped puzzle) and megaminx (a 12-sided, pentagonal-shaped puzzle). Of course, I didn't expect my times to be very flawless or to place myself to the semi-finals as I was about a year and 2 months into my cubing journey.

When I stepped into the LT for the first time, I was awe-struck by seeing so many speedcubers playing with their speedcubes where plastic noises emanated throughout the entire LT. It went within a span of 2 days, and the most exciting part was on the 2nd day, where my best friends were there to support me and my other friend in the competition! At the same time, me and my friends were proudly wearing the 'PHCuber' t-shirt because I wanted to represent my brand proudly to everyone to let them know I'm running PixelHyperCuber (PHCuber)! At the same time, I am very grateful to my parents for helping me support me in the competition, even though it was one of my interests at the time.

However, the video I made wasn't really the best as there were notable flaws in it — how I censored my face (due to my parents not recommending me to reveal my face at the time), the heavily shaky footage, and trimming/speeding up entire solves (but the video's title included 'All Solves…' however it might cause viewers to skip). Looking back at the video, I pointed out every single piece of the 'imperfections' there was on the original video, so that I can get a lot better to work on my Singapore 333 Cube Competition video. 

###### Singapore 333 Cube Competition 2016

This was my 2nd ever competition, and I was incredibly surprised to know that Mats Valk, a well-known Dutch professional speedcuber, was also participating in it! Mats Valk was one of the best speedcubers in the world and he was most well-known for getting a world record (former - but still super memorable) 3x3 single of 5.55 seconds back in 2013 in a Dutch speedcubing competition (Zonhoven Open 2013). The main reason why Mats Valk wanted to participate in such a random speedcube competition in Singapore (and knowing that the event only caters to only 3x3 events), was mainly because he wanted to advertise his latest 3x3, the Valk 3, by travelling to numerous countries across Asia and Oceania like Indonesia, Taiwan and Australia to compete. Knowing that Mats Valk was also participating in the event, I was ecstatic to even see him in person and I couldn't wait to see how he performed in the competition!

When I arrived at the competition venue, I was invited with the same landscape of cubers, as the whole LT was flooded with numerous speedcubers who were constantly flicking with their speedcubes. I got to meet a group of the same speedcubers that I met at the previous competition, and I even experienced one fan who had the confidence to come up to me and ask for a photo! When my mother saw this happening, she instantly felt very proud of me that people were recognizing me in real life, however, I didn't fully understand how much of a milestone this was because I constantly compared my channel (300+ subscribers at the time) with big YouCubers like JRCuber, CrazyBadCuber and RedKB who all have 100k+ subscribers. Looking back at that moment, I would say that it was quite a milestone for me as there are numerous people who have 10-100x my subscriber count who never had this opportunity (by the way, I was wearing my channel's t-shirt which helped in the recognition).

My solves had improved since the last competition — my first round average was sub-15 seconds, which was good enough to qualify me to compete in the 2nd round. However, my 2nd round got worse by about 1 second and my times fluctuated quite a lot, but the good thing was that I got a 11.55 single and I was decently happy about it at the time since I usually average around 14-15 seconds at the time. My one-handed performance was surprisingly ok, considering I rarely do it and that I'm quite terrible at it, however I managed to fulfil my goal of getting a sub-40 average!

After my solves, I was walking in circles while waiting for Mats Valk to sign my YJ GuanLong, because to be frank, I had a bit of social anxiety where I feel anxious when I approach people I've never met before. However, after he finished attending to a guy, I politely asked him for his autograph and gladly signed my cube on the middle piece. After which, I replied "Thanks Mats, good job on the solve!", because I want to acknowledge that he was one of the most elite speedcubers at the time. And after asking him whether he could break sub-6 (a solve strictly under 6 seconds — 5.xx), he replied "You need a bit of luck on that", after which he proceeded to conclude the entire competition with a 5.68 single during the finals!

After spending countless hours and days editing, videoing and scripting my competition vlog, I was proud that I have improved a lot compared to the Singapore Open 2016 video I made back in February. I used my DSLR to film the best possible angles in the entire competition venue for viewers (local and international) to have a glimpse of how the Singaporean cubing scene was like back in those days. There were still some occasional mistakes here and there, but it doesn't really undermine the video very badly. I was not only glad that my day at the competition was splendid, but I was genuinely very happy and surprised that Mats Valk joined an official WCA competition in Singapore in the first place!

#### The sudden downfall of PixelHyperCuber

Right at the end of the year, I was preparing for my Sec 2 exams, where the workload progressively increased due to my exam preparation. As a result, after I made my Valk 3 first impressions video, I had to make an status update to the channel about why I haven't really been uploading so much due to exams. At the same time, I was running my Shengshou Aurora Megaminx giveaway too. So, after my exams were over, I announced the winner and I finally made an in-depth review of the Valk 3. I have decided that I wanted a sponsorship from Cubezz, which I did eventually get. So they sent me a random budget cube for me to test and review. 

However, as I was dealing with burnout due to the hate comments I might get on the channel, as well as putting myself to unrealistically high standards for my videos, I decided that I had enough. At the same time, knowing that I had put in so much effort into many of the videos I made on the channel, it really hurts me that there would be some people who would lash out on me by sending hate comments on my channel, where one of the most notable ones included 'unoriginal channel name go kys'. After processing that comment in my head repeatedly, it felt so contradictory because why would someone say something like that knowing that I had sacrificed my free time to make quality videos for my viewers to enjoy. At the same time, I also had to balance my school commitments because I take pride in school, and this was just one of my other hobbies of mine.

This made me realize that I was very fortunate that I had been raised in a nurturing environment, where hard work instantly translates to validation and amazement from the people that recognized my work. Even though I sort of knew back in my head that I couldn't control what others think of me, I stopped uploading because I developed a slight phobia of putting myself online as the internet is filled with people with many different types of personalities. As an adolescent at the time, my brain was not emotionally mature and developed enough to understand that we really couldn't please everyone, and this person might be dealing with other issues himself that he felt the need to last out at channels like mine. Even after deleting that nasty comment, the pain still lingered onto me because I simply couldn't understand why someone would make a comment like this even though I put in a lot of hard work into making it.

Long after I stopped uploading, I constantly felt an insane amount of guilt about the fact that I did not trust and make promises to my loyal viewers of my channel. Knowing that I was about to progress to Secondary 3, I had to cope with the changes of the subjects I was doing, as they were progressively getting more advanced compared to my Sec 2 coursework. As a result, I had to prioritize my academics a lot more compared to my Sec 2 days. If I continued making more videos, my time would have been drained by a lot and I would not even have the time to focus deeply on my academics. At the same time, I would not even be prioritizing my health and I might sacrifice some of my sleep hours just to resolve numerous issues like editing and shooting inconsistencies. 

As a result, I wrote a long personal blog (about >1000 words) about why I haven't been uploading on my channel as well as my cubing story, solely because speedcubing is one part of my identity and I loved to share my passion to the world. I was planning to share my blog on one of my posts, however, after thinking about it carefully about whether I should hit 'Post', I was glad that I didn't because it reveals sensitive info about my friends' names as well as my familial interactions. Despite the abrupt pause in my YouTube channel, I was happy that I managed to inspire numerous people within that niche, even though I was still considered a micro-creator. I am thankful of all the genuine support I was given to my channel and IG page, online and offline. At the same time, I am very thankful to both my parents for financially supporting my interests at the time, and I'm fortunate that I had the opportunity to learn numerous skillsets at such a young age that might be useful in my future pursuits. 

#### My Post-YT Takeaways

Now, that I'm currently doing an NTU Computer Science degree, the skills I picked up when I was running PixelHyperCube and PixelHyperCuber have translated well:

| Video-making Skill | Software Engineer Equivalent |
| --------- | -------- |
| Editing videos | Debugging lines of code |
| Scripting | Writing code for a program/web app |
| Video Idea Generation | Deciding which program/web app to build |
| Following algorithmic demands | Looking at modern frameworks and leveraging them to the fullest |

Looking back, it was an awesome journey and I definitely enjoyed my time running PixelHyperCube and PixelHyperCuber. The greatest achievement I got was that I grew a micro-community of twisty puzzle and speedcube enthusiasts to my pages and that I have been recognized by a sizable number of people for the work I did. Even though I was definitely not very popular compared to the big YouTubers out there, I was proud that I was brave enough to share my unique passions to the world despite the subtle hostility that existed online. 

Right now as I'm writing this blog, I'm still speedcubing, but I'm not the most competitive out there as I mainly do it as a hobby during my free time!
